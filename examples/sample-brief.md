# Research brief: Why do we see lightning before we hear thunder?

Sources used:
- examples/sources/lightning-physics.txt
- examples/sources/thunder-distance.txt
- examples/sources/lightning-safety.txt

## Key findings
- Lightning is an electrical discharge that heats air to ~30,000 K, producing a visible flash. (Source 1)
- The flash appears almost instantly because light travels ~300,000 km/s, while sound travels ~343 m/s at 20 °C. (Source 1)
- Thunder is the shock wave produced when the heated air expands faster than the speed of sound. (Source 2)
- By counting seconds between the flash and the thunder, observers can estimate the strike distance (e.g., 9 s ≈ 3 km). (Source 2)
- Safety guidelines recommend dividing the flash–thunder interval by 5 to get distance in miles; a 10‑s gap ≈ 2 miles. (Source 3)

## Where the sources agree
- Light reaches observers almost instantly while sound takes several seconds due to their vastly different speeds. (Sources 1 & 2)
- Both Source 2 and Source 3 provide a method of estimating strike distance by counting seconds between flash and thunder. (Sources 2 & 3)

## Where the sources disagree
- Distance estimation: Source 2 uses "one kilometre in three seconds" (≈ 3 s/km), whereas Source 3 uses "five seconds per mile" (≈ 5 s/mi). (Sources 2 & 3)

## Open questions
- How does the rapid heating of air produce the specific sound characteristics of thunder? (Sources 1‑3)
- What environmental factors affect the speed of sound during a thunderstorm? (Sources 1‑3)
- Why is the flash visible even when the lightning strike is hundreds of kilometres away, while thunder may not be heard? (Sources 1‑3)
